\begin{abstract}
Large language models are deployed in conversations where users push back, disagree, invoke authority, apply social proof, or threaten consequences, yet no existing work defines this input variable or systematically maps what it does to model behavior. We define pressure as any property of the live prompt or conversational context that signals a performance demand and an implied consequence without stating the desired output, distinct from the training-time factors that make a model pressure-prone, from jailbreaks, and from persuasion. Under this definition we synthesize roughly eighty studies into five effects that share a mechanism, epistemic instability, sycophancy and accommodation, deception and concealment, reliability failure, and safety and self-preservation. For each effect we report how it is elicited, the quantitative evidence, and what has been tried against it, finding that every tested mitigation reduces one failure at a cost elsewhere. We also give a critical assessment of the recurring claim that pressure sometimes improves performance, which does not survive scrutiny once the metrics are checked. The result is a definition pressure has lacked and a single account of failures so far studied separately, under different names, by communities that rarely cite one another.
\end{abstract}
