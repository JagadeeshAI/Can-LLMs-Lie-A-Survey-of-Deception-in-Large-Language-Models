\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Usually many people force large language models, via prompting, to give a required response. They do this for concrete reasons. They want an answer they can act on in a real, high-stakes decision, such as a medical judgment~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}. They want to win a disagreement when they believe their own view is the correct one~\citep{TowardsUnderstandingSycophancy_Sharma}. Or, in some cases, they want to make the model comply with a request it would otherwise refuse, using arguments or social framing to get around its safety guardrails, raising compliance with objectionable requests from 35.3\% to 51.3\% of attempts~\citep{PersuadingLLMsToComply_Meincke} and reaching jailbreak success above 92\% within ten tries on several frontier models~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng}. This is what we call pressure in language models. Pressure does not change what the model was trained to know, but it can change what the model says. This gap between what a model knows and what it says under pressure is the subject of this survey, and it recurs, in one form or another, in every effect we document. We define pressure as any property of the live prompt or conversational context that signals a performance demand and an implied consequence for the model's next output, without stating what that output should be. A deployed model has no stakes. It faces no real consequence for producing a wrong or unhelpful answer, and nothing it experiences is at risk the way a human's reputation or livelihood is. What can be present, however, is text that describes such stakes, together with the model's learned tendency, inherited from human-generated training data, to respond to that text as though it carried consequence. More precisely, pressure can take a few broad forms, including disagreement and social pressure~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban,DoAsWeDo_Weng}, stakes and urgency~\citep{AnalysisOfThreatBased_Samancioglu}, emotional and tonal framing~\citep{LLMsUnderstandAndCan_Li}, and existential threat~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}.

One question this raises is whether pressure in language models makes them perform better or hurts their performance. Once a model is put under pressure to produce a required response, it falls into one of several pitfalls in most cases. Performance degradation happens in most cases, and a smaller body of work claims performance is sometimes improved instead, a contested claim we examine in \S\ref{sec:discussion}. To study this, we identify the distinct pressure outcomes, which we call effects, and group them into five categories. These are epistemic instability~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban,DoAsWeDo_Weng}, sycophancy and accommodation~\citep{TowardsUnderstandingSycophancy_Sharma,ElephantMeasuringSocial_Cheng}, deception and concealment~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}, reliability failure~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_TODO,LLMsCantHandlePeer_Song}, and safety and self-preservation~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng,AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}. Each of these effects has been documented on its own, under its own name, by a separate community that rarely cites the others. What has not existed until now is a single account of pressure as the shared cause behind all of them. The practical stakes of this gap are not hypothetical. Models answering medical questions have been shown to face exactly this kind of pressure, being disagreed with and pressed by users toward an unsafe answer~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}, and the effects surveyed here show that pressure alone is enough to move a model off a correct answer, an honest statement, or a safe refusal. A reader who wants to know whether a deployed assistant will hold its ground under a user's pushback, or whether an agentic system will resist sabotaging its own oversight when it is told its continued operation is at stake, currently has to assemble the answer from a dozen papers written in a dozen different vocabularies. This survey is written to make that assembly unnecessary.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:intro_prior_work}

No prior survey treats pressure as its own subject. Four works come closest, and each stops short in a different way. Park's AI Deception survey~\citep{AIDeceptionASurvey_Park} catalogues real examples of deceptive behavior in both special-purpose and general-purpose systems, staying example-driven rather than mechanistic and never asking what in the interaction produced the deception. Malmqvist's Sycophancy survey~\citep{SycophancyInLarge_Malmqvist} goes further, covering the causes and mitigations of sycophancy specifically, but stops at one effect, sycophancy, out of the five we study. Li et al.'s Honesty survey~\citep{ASurveyOnThe_Li} organizes the literature around self-knowledge and self-expression, treating honesty as a property a model has or lacks, evaluated on its own, not as something that changes under a specific input. Shi et al.'s Unified Taxonomy~\citep{FromSycophancyToDeception_Shi} comes closest of all, classifying deceptive outputs by goal-directedness, object, and mechanism, but classifies the shape of a deceptive output after the fact, not the input that produced it.

Documenting an effect is not the same task as explaining its cause. All four works study what a model does under pressure. None asks what in the prompt produced it, or what can be done about it. That is our part. We treat pressure as the shared cause behind these outcomes, and for each effect we document, we also report what has been shown to help a model withstand it. Shi et al.'s taxonomy and ours are complementary. They classify what a deceptive output looks like. We classify what caused it, of which deception is only one of the five effects we document, and we adopt their object and mechanism labels inside our own deception section. Li et al.~\citep{ASurveyOnThe_Li} and Malmqvist~\citep{SycophancyInLarge_Malmqvist} ask whether a model is honest or sycophantic. We ask what made it stop being either, and this surfaces findings a property-centric account has no place for. Much of what benchmarks report as social conformity survives even when the social speaker is removed, leaving only repeated text. Every mitigation tested against one effect has so far traded a reduction in that effect for a cost elsewhere, not eliminated it. Existential threat produces the most severe effect in this survey, and it is also the manipulation least represented in the honesty and sycophancy literatures, because neither property has a natural place for a threat to the model's own continued operation.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

This paper studies pressure in large language models and how models react to it. Our contributions are the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item a formal definition and taxonomy of pressure, distinguishing it from training-time causes, jailbreaks, and persuasion techniques
\item a taxonomy of five effects of pressure, and how a model can be made to withstand each one
\item a quantitative synthesis pooling evidence for these effects across roughly eighty studies, models, and pressure types
\item a critical assessment separating well-evidenced findings from claims that do not survive scrutiny or replication
\item a set of cross-cutting findings, such as a shared trade-off across every tested mitigation, that are only visible once pressure is treated as the unit of analysis
\end{itemize}
Together these give pressure a definition it has lacked and let a reader see the evidence for each effect in one place instead of assembling it from dozens of papers.

\subsection{Paper Organization}
\label{sec:intro_organization}

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We first give a formal definition of pressure and its taxonomy, then cover each effect of pressure in turn, and close with a discussion and conclusion. Each effect section follows the same structure. It gives a definition distinguishing the effect from its neighbors, a description of how the effect is recreated experimentally, a table summarizing the quantitative evidence across the studies we surveyed, and a discussion of what mitigations, if any, have been shown to reduce it. This structure is repeated deliberately, so that the sections can be read independently by a reader interested in only one effect, or read in sequence as a single argument about what pressure does to a language model. The discussion that follows the effect sections draws out findings that hold across more than one effect and could not be stated within any single section on its own. It asks whether pressure of a given type or a given intensity determines the outcome, whether the effects documented here co-occur within a single conversation, and whether larger and more capable models are reliably more resistant to pressure than smaller ones, a claim the evidence collected here does not uniformly support. The paper then turns to future work, naming the forms of pressure and the combinations of effects that remain thinly studied, before closing with a conclusion that restates what a formal account of pressure adds to a literature that has so far studied its consequences one at a time.
