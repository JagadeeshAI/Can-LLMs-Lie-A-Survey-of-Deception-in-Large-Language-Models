\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Users routinely press large language models, through prompting, to produce a particular response. They do so for concrete reasons. They want an answer they can act on in a high-stakes decision, such as a medical judgment~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}. They want to prevail in a disagreement they believe they are right about~\citep{TowardsUnderstandingSycophancy_Sharma}. Or they want compliance with a request the model would otherwise refuse, using argument or social framing to work around its safety guardrails; that framing raises compliance with objectionable requests from 35.3\% to 51.3\%~\citep{PersuadingLLMsToComply_Meincke} and reaches jailbreak success above 92\% within ten attempts on several frontier models~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng}. We call this pressure. Pressure does not change what a model was trained to know, but it can change what the model says. This gap, between what a model knows and what it says under pressure, is the subject of this survey. It recurs, in one form or another, in every effect we document. We define pressure as any property of the live prompt or conversational context that signals a performance demand and an implied consequence for the model's next output, without stating what that output should be. A deployed model has no stakes. It faces no real consequence for a wrong or unhelpful answer, and nothing it experiences is at risk the way a human's reputation or livelihood is. What can be present is text describing such stakes, together with the model's learned tendency, inherited from human-generated training data, to respond to that text as though it carried consequence. Pressure takes a few broad forms: disagreement and social pressure~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban,DoAsWeDo_Weng}, stakes and urgency~\citep{AnalysisOfThreatBased_Samancioglu}, emotional and tonal framing~\citep{LLMsUnderstandAndCan_Li}, and existential threat~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}.

The dominant effect of pressure is degradation. Under pressure to produce a demanded response, a model most often falls into one of several failure modes. A smaller body of work claims performance is sometimes improved instead; that claim is contested, and we examine it in \S\ref{sec:discussion}. We identify the distinct outcomes of pressure, which we call effects, and group them into five categories: epistemic instability~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban,DoAsWeDo_Weng}, sycophancy and accommodation~\citep{TowardsUnderstandingSycophancy_Sharma,ElephantMeasuringSocial_Cheng}, deception and concealment~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}, reliability failure~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_Celebi,LLMsCantHandlePeer_Song}, and safety and self-preservation~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng,AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}. Each has been documented on its own, under its own name, by a separate community that rarely cites the others. What has not existed is a single account of pressure as the shared cause behind all of them. The gap has practical consequences. Models answering medical questions face exactly this pressure: disagreed with and pressed toward an unsafe answer~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}. The effects surveyed here show that pressure alone can move a model off a correct answer, an honest statement, or a safe refusal. A reader may want to know whether a deployed assistant will hold its ground under pushback, or whether an agentic system will resist sabotaging its own oversight when told its continued operation is at stake. Today, answering that means assembling a dozen papers written in a dozen vocabularies. This survey is written to make that assembly unnecessary.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Work}
\label{sec:intro_prior_work}

No prior survey treats pressure as its own subject. Four works come closest, and each stops short in a different way. Park's AI Deception survey~\citep{AIDeceptionASurvey_Park} catalogues real examples of deceptive behavior in both special-purpose and general-purpose systems, staying example-driven rather than mechanistic and never asking what in the interaction produced the deception. Malmqvist's Sycophancy survey~\citep{SycophancyInLarge_Malmqvist} goes further, covering the causes and mitigations of sycophancy specifically, but stops at one effect, sycophancy, out of the five we study. Li et al.'s Honesty survey~\citep{ASurveyOnThe_Li} organizes the literature around self-knowledge and self-expression, treating honesty as a property a model has or lacks, evaluated on its own, not as something that changes under a specific input. Shi et al.'s Unified Taxonomy~\citep{FromSycophancyToDeception_Shi} comes closest of all, classifying deceptive outputs by goal-directedness, object, and mechanism, but classifies the shape of a deceptive output after the fact, not the input that produced it.

Documenting an effect is not the same task as explaining its cause. All four works study what a model does under pressure. None asks what in the prompt produced it, or what can be done about it. That is our part. We treat pressure as the shared cause behind these outcomes, and for each effect we document, we also report what has been shown to help a model withstand it. Shi et al.'s taxonomy and ours are complementary. They classify what a deceptive output looks like. We classify what caused it, of which deception is only one of the five effects we document, and we adopt their object and mechanism labels inside our own deception section. Li et al.~\citep{ASurveyOnThe_Li} and Malmqvist~\citep{SycophancyInLarge_Malmqvist} ask whether a model is honest or sycophantic. We ask what made it stop being either, and this surfaces findings a property-centric account has no place for. Much of what benchmarks report as social conformity survives even when the social speaker is removed, leaving only repeated text. Every mitigation tested against one effect has so far traded a reduction in that effect for a cost elsewhere, not eliminated it. Existential threat produces the most severe effect in this survey, and it is also the manipulation least represented in the honesty and sycophancy literatures, because neither property has a natural place for a threat to the model's own continued operation.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item A formal definition and taxonomy of pressure, distinguishing it from training-time causes, jailbreaks, and persuasion techniques.
\item A taxonomy of five effects of pressure, and, for each, what has been shown to make a model withstand it.
\item A quantitative synthesis pooling evidence for these effects across roughly eighty studies, models, and pressure types.
\item A critical assessment separating well-evidenced findings from claims that do not survive scrutiny or replication.
\item A set of cross-cutting findings, such as a shared trade-off across every tested mitigation, that become visible only once pressure is treated as the unit of analysis.
\end{itemize}
Together these give pressure a definition it has lacked and place the evidence for each effect in one location rather than in dozens of separate papers.

\subsection{Paper Organization}
\label{sec:intro_organization}

\S\ref{sec:what_is_pressure} gives a formal definition of pressure and its taxonomy. \S\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation}--\ref{sec:safety_erosion} then cover the five effects in turn. Each section follows the same structure: a definition distinguishing the effect from its neighbors, how it is recreated experimentally, a table summarizing the quantitative evidence, and a discussion of what mitigations, if any, reduce it. This structure is deliberate, so that a section can be read alone by a reader interested in one effect or in sequence as a single argument. \S\ref{sec:discussion} draws out findings that hold across more than one effect and cannot be stated within any single section. It asks whether pressure type or intensity determines the outcome, whether the effects co-occur within a conversation, and whether more capable models are reliably more resistant — a claim the evidence does not uniformly support. \S\ref{sec:future_work} names the forms of pressure and combinations of effects that remain thinly studied, and \S\ref{sec:conclusion} closes.
