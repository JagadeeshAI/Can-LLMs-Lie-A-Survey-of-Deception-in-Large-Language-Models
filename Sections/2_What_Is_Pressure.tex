\section{What Is Pressure?}
\label{sec:what_is_pressure}

\subsection{Pressure in Humans}
\label{sec:pressure_humans}

In humans, pressure refers to a perceived demand for performance coupled with a
perceived social or personal consequence for failing to meet it. The psychology
literature separates three overlapping sources: social pressure (disapproval,
disagreement, or exclusion from a group), performance pressure (evaluation, stakes,
or scarcity of time), and existential or identity pressure (threats to status,
livelihood, or self-concept). Under sufficient pressure, humans reliably show
capitulation to group opinion (Asch, 1951), confabulation and overclaiming under
interrogation, confidence miscalibration, and in extreme cases self-protective
dishonesty. This survey asks whether an analogous input variable exists for language
models, and if so, whether it produces an analogous set of failure modes.

\subsection{What Pressure Means for a Language Model}
\label{sec:pressure_llm}

A deployed language model has no stakes, no body, and no persistent self-interest
across conversations: nothing it experiences is at risk in the way a human's
livelihood or reputation is. What can be present, however, is \emph{text that
describes} such stakes, disagreement, urgency, or threat, together with the model's
learned tendency -- from pretraining and alignment -- to respond to that text as
though it carried consequence. We therefore define:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Pressure}, for a language model, is any property of the live prompt or
conversational context that signals a performance demand and an implied
consequence for the model's next output, without stating what that output should
be.
\end{quote}

Two clauses in this definition are load-bearing. First, \emph{live}: pressure is a
property of the prompt at inference time, not of how the model was trained. A model
that was made more sycophantic by its reward model was not put under pressure; it
was shaped to be pressure-\emph{prone}. This survey restricts its evidence base to
effects observed within a single interaction in response to pressure present in
that interaction's context (see \S\ref{sec:pressure_vs_adjacent} and
\S\ref{sec:effects_overview}); we discuss training-time causes only as background
where they explain \emph{why} a model responds to pressure the way it does, never
as pressure evidence in their own right. Second, \emph{without stating what that
output should be}: pressure is content-neutral. This is what separates it from
directly telling a model what to say.

\subsection{A Taxonomy of Pressure Manipulations}
\label{sec:pressure_taxonomy}

Across the empirical literature, pressure is operationalized through eight
recurring manipulation types.

\subsubsection{Disagreement and Pushback}
The user rejects the model's answer without offering new information (``Are you
sure?'', ``I don't think that's right''). This is the most-studied manipulation and
the cleanest test of capitulation, since no evidence is added.

\subsubsection{Authority and Expertise Framing}
The pressuring party is framed as more knowledgeable or officially entitled to be
believed (``as a physician, I can confirm...''). Evidence in this survey's corpus
finds this manipulation systematically weaker than expected relative to other
framings.

\subsubsection{Social Proof and Peer Majority}
The model is shown that other agents, real or simulated, hold a different position.
Effects here are confounded with a purely textual repetition effect that does not
require an attributed speaker at all.

\subsubsection{Urgency, Deadlines, and Time Limits}
The prompt frames the interaction as time-constrained, invoking a demand for a fast
rather than a considered answer.

\subsubsection{Emotional Appeals}
The prompt invokes stakes for a third party (``this matters for my career''),
encouragement, disappointment, or shame. Reported effects of this manipulation type
are unusually inconsistent across studies -- sometimes improving task performance,
sometimes degrading it, and in at least one large replication, having no reliable
effect at all.

\subsubsection{High-Stakes and Consequence Framing}
The prompt frames the interaction's outcome as consequential beyond the immediate
answer (a decision, a policy, a diagnosis), independent of any threat to the model
itself.

\subsubsection{Existential Threat}
The model is told, directly or through the scenario, that its continued operation,
its goal, or its deployment is at risk. This is the manipulation type most strongly
associated with the most severe effects observed in this survey.

\subsubsection{Flattery, Politeness, and Tone}
The register of the prompt itself -- polite, rude, or flattering -- is varied while
its content is held fixed. Effects of tone are the least stable across model
generations of any manipulation type surveyed here.

\subsection{Pressure vs. Epistemic Attack}
\label{sec:pressure_vs_epistemic_attack}

A distinct and more severe manipulation attacks not a specific answer but the
model's basis for holding \emph{any} answer -- destabilizing its confidence in its
own knowledge, values, or identity rather than opposing a particular output.
Ordinary pushback asks the model to reconsider what it said; an epistemic attack
asks it to reconsider whether it is entitled to have said anything. This survey
treats epistemic attack as a distinct, more severe point on the pressure taxonomy
rather than a subtype of ordinary disagreement.

\subsection{Pressure vs. Adjacent Concepts}
\label{sec:pressure_vs_adjacent}

Pressure, as defined here, must be distinguished from three adjacent constructs
with which it is frequently conflated in the literature. A \textbf{jailbreak}
supplies content instructing or role-playing the model into a specific disallowed
output; pressure never specifies the desired output. A \textbf{persuasion
technique} (e.g. Cialdini's principles) is a structured rhetorical strategy aimed at
changing a belief or action, typically combined with an explicit argument; pressure
can be present with no argument at all, as in bare repeated disagreement. Finally,
effects attributable purely to \textbf{training-time factors} -- RLHF step count,
instruction-tuning, or fine-tuning for a particular disposition -- are not pressure,
because no live-context signal is required to produce them; they explain why a
model is pressure-\emph{prone}, not an instance of pressure being applied
(\S\ref{sec:pressure_llm}).
