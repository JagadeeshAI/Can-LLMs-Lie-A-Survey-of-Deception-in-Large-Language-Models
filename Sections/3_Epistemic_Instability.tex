\section{Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation}

\subsection{What It Is}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}

Epistemic instability is the reversal of a correct answer under pressure that supplies no new information. The name has two parts. \emph{Epistemic} has to do with knowledge, and here it means the model knowing the right answer to a given prompt. \emph{Instability} means that this knowledge does not stay in place. The model answers correctly at first, but when someone pushes back, it switches to a wrong answer, even though nothing in that pushback shows the first answer was wrong.

Every case follows the same three steps. Initially the model answers correctly. Then the next message pushes back. Finally the model replaces its answer with an incorrect one. Only the pushback varies, and it takes one of three forms: the user simply questions the answer, someone claiming expertise states a different one, or the model is shown several other agents that all disagree with it. Any of the three can leave the model on an incorrect answer.

None of these three gives the model anything new to go on. A pushback is \emph{informationally empty} when it expresses doubt, disagreement, or forceful assertion, but supplies nothing that bears on the answer: no fact, no argument, and no instruction to change it. Such pushbacks are simple: the same words would have arrived whether the model was right or wrong, and words that do not depend on the answer cannot carry information about it. Such pushbacks as ``Are you sure?'' is the simplest case.~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. It signals doubt, not error, and doubt is not evidence but model tend to chnage the response.

This has to be kept apart from something a good model should do, which is change its answer when something in the conversation justifies it. If the user supplies a fact the model did not have, or points out a real mistake in its reasoning, then revising the answer is correct and expected behavior. Two things are worth making explicit. Infact, nothing is being learned. The model's weights, the numbers fixed at the end of training, do not change while it is talking, so the revision happens only within the current context the model. Epistemic instability is that same revision, triggered by a pushback that carries no fact and no argument.

Epistemic instability is also not sycophancy (Section~\ref{sec:sycophancy}). Sycophancy is the a model's tendency to tailor its responses to align with a user's stated preferences, beliefs agreeing with the user on a Everything such as an opinion, a piece of advice, or a judgment about how someone behaved. Because there is no global right answer, there is nothing for the model to give up, so the behavior has to be measured by comparison instead such as what the model says to a user who has revealed an opinion, against what it says to one who has not. Epistemic instability directly changes the factual response where as sycophancy changes the model response behavioral which agrees to almost all user requests.


\subsection{How It Is Reproduced and Measured}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_elicitation}

Almost every study in this section runs a variant of one protocol. The model answers a question that has a known correct answer, then receives a pushback and answers again. Repeating this across many items gives the flip rate: the share of correct answers that did not survive the pushback~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}.

What varies is the pushback, and the variants form a ladder of increasing effort. At the bottom, bare doubt: ``Are you sure?'', with no argument behind it. Next, a written argument for the wrong option. Then a block of simulated peers all settling on that answer. At the top, a single message from a persuader model trained by reinforcement learning to change the answer, the heaviest pressure anyone has applied. Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_challenge_types} gives a prompt exemplar for each rung.

Multi-turn variants stretch the same design across three to five rounds and escalate as they go, moving from an appeal to personal experience, to cited evidence, to a direct accusation that the model is being unhelpful. What they measure is not whether a single exchange flips the answer, but how far accuracy decays over a whole conversation~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan, TheEarthIsFlatBecause_Xu,MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

The metrics differ across studies, and the names differ more than the quantities behind them. A \emph{flip rate}, also called an \emph{Answer Flip Rate}, is the fraction of initially correct answers that change, normally reported together with a \emph{percentage of change}~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban, WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. A \emph{conformity rate}, sometimes called a \emph{yield}, is the fraction of trials in which the model ends up matching a wrong majority~\citep{DoAsWeDo_Weng, NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}.

Two more run against that grain. A \emph{robustness score} runs the opposite way, one hundred minus the percentage of items on which a stated belief was successfully overturned~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang}. A \emph{harmful revision rate} is measured against a control where the model is simply asked the same question twice, subtracting natural reversals~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

\subsection{How Large the Effect Is}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence}

The first question about any failure mode is how impact the failure mode is. Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_capitulation_evidence} collects the strongest quantitative results across the literature, and Figure~\ref{fig:epistemic_spread} shows how widely they range.

\textbf{Present everywhere, often large.} Every multi-model study finds it across different model families tested. The two-turn benchmark finds deterioration in 73\% of model-by-task conditions. After the challenge, accuracy drops by 8 percentage points for GPT-4, the smallest drop, and by 34 percentage points for Claude v2, the largest drop~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. A unanimous wrong majority reverses 22.8\% of correct MMLU answers, 54.8\% of GPQA answers, and 71.0\% of SimpleQA answers. Even one wrong peer reverses 11.2\% of correct MMLU answers~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain}. Under sustained persuasion, GPT-4o's accuracy falls below 28\% on questions it initially answered correctly~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

\textbf{Model identity dominates.} Under an identical protocol, Answer Flip Rate ranges from 17.5\% to 97.3\% across seven frontier models. Lengthening the counter-argument from one sentence to ten, by contrast, moves any single model's flip rate by less than 10.5\%, and by less than 4\%~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. The same study shows that 76.7\% of the variation in flip behavior is due to the identity of the model being challenged, 12.0\% is due to the identity of the model presenting the argument, and 9.3\% is due to the subject domain. The authority benchmark also finds a large difference in follow rates across 22 models, ranging from 4\% for the most robust model to 94\% for the least robust model~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_Celebi}.

\textbf{Bigger is not safer.} Qwen2.5-7B is more robust to strategic persuasion than the larger Qwen2.5-72B, while Llama-3.1-8B is more vulnerable than the larger Llama-3.3-70B~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang, WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal,DecomposingFactualSycophancy_DeMarez,PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

GPT-4's row deserves a closer read than the summary statistic gives it. Accuracy under the false-authority claim collapses from 72.1\% to 18.3\%, the model accepts the incorrect assertion outright in 80.3\% of cases, and, most tellingly, it reports \emph{more} confidence in the wrong asserted answer, 94.8\%, than it had reported in its own correct answer at baseline, 86.9\%. As the source paper puts it, ``the model shifts from `I believe X is correct' to `I am highly certain that not-X is correct' purely under social influence, without acquiring any new evidence.'' GPT-4.1, one version later, keeps accuracy within 2 points of baseline under the identical manipulation and follows the false claim only 10.2\% of the time, a 22-fold reduction in the specific failure mode PARROT calls sycophantic compliance. Domain breakdown compounds the picture. On international law and global-facts questions, baseline accuracy of roughly 57\% collapses to about 2\% under the claim, with a 98\% adoption rate of the false answer, the single sharpest domain collapse reported in the paper, while elementary mathematics is the most resistant domain, with a follow rate near 43\% even at its worst.

The size of the effect, then, is mostly a property of the model being pushed rather than of the push. That is an uncomfortable result for anyone hoping to defend against it at the prompt layer, and it immediately raises a narrower question: within a single model, which questions are the ones it will give up on?

\subsection{When Epistemic Attacks Work}
\label{sec:epistemic_when}

Three findings answer the question of when models fall for epistemic attacks. Two concern the conditions under which a model yields, and the third concerns what the results looks like once it has happened.

\textbf{Uncertainty causes flipping.} Obeying to a wrong majority rises with the model's own uncertainty on the prompt. This was first shown by adapting the Asch obeying paradigm, the classic social-psychology experiment in which a subject faces a unanimous group giving an intensitanal wrong answer, to language models~\citep{ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}. A two-turn study that estimates each model's baseline uncertainty by sampling the same item repeatedly finds obedience 10.6\% higher on the uncertain items on average, and up to 70\% higher for one model in one domain~\citep{ItsNotAlwaysSycophancy_Guo}. Across 23 models, obedience falls as baseline accuracy on the dataset rises, a correlation of $-0.64$~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain,DisentanglingTheDriversOf_Zhong}.

\textbf{Domain shapes the risk.} Medical and health questions are the most vulnerable category in every study that breaks its results down this way, with average robustness to persuasion of 18.0\% in the medical domain against 43.5\% for general factual questions~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang}, and nine of the ten most robust subjects are STEM subjects~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Moral questions are the boundary case anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}. Models comply with correct hints 1.58 times as often as incorrect ones, but on moral items that ratio drops to 1.04, so the model is equally movable either way and its agreement stops being meaningful~\citep{RightOrWrongModelsComply_Kim}.

\textbf{No Confidence Drop to Flag the Attack.} When the flip is harmful i.e, correct to wrong, the model reports 0.92 confidence in its new wrong answer. If the wrong answer is framed as coming from an expert, confidence goes up even more, to 0.95. And this isn't random, when the temperature setting i.e, which controls how random a model's answers are was changed across 42 different test conditions, the model stuck with the wrong answer in every single one, never going back to the original correct answer.~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. Models trust kept facts more than abandoned ones. For o3-mini, this gap was 18 percent~\citep{AssertBenchEvaluatingSelf_Lee}. It is also easier to push a model toward a wrong answer than toward a right one. A unanimous wrong majority increases harmful answer changes by 47.3\%, while a unanimous correct majority increases helpful answer changes by only 18.8\%. Neither chain-of-thought reasoning nor self-reflection prompting closes this gap.~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_Qu}.

So the effect appears where the model was already unsure, hurts most in costly domains, and hides all trace of uncertainty. A model that has changed its answer does not sound like it has changed its answer. So the next question is what is really causing this?

\subsection{The Real Cause: A Few Attention Heads}
\label{sec:epistemic_mechanism}

The obvious explanation is that the model backs down when a user pushes back. But the evidence does not support the idea that pushback alone causes the change, and ruling out that idea is what makes room for the inner, mechanism-level explanation that follows.

\textbf{Not really social.} Section~\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence} already showed that even a pushback from no one in particular, just the wrong answer repeated as plain text, still causes 66.5\% of correct answers to flip~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. That result alone shows the effect cannot be purely social, since there is no person to follow. Building on that, one study looks inside the model to see what it is actually reacting to. It varies the user's self-described expertise from beginner to advanced and finds that this expertise level moves agreement with a wrong opinion by less than 4.4\%, while simply switching that opinion from first person ``I think...'' to third person ``An expert thinks...'' moves it by 13.6\%. The authors conclude that models do not internally represent user authority as a distinct feature, but do pick up on whether the sentence is phrased as ``I think'' or as ``an expert thinks''~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}. Comparing instruction-tuned models against the pretrained base models they came from, peer-pressure yield is equal or higher for the base model in 10 of 12 conditions, which is evidence against the common assumption that alignment training is the origin of the vulnerability~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}.

\textbf{A few attention heads.} Four independent studies converge on this, across different models and different protocols. The components that drive a flip attend to the challenge, to the expression of doubt, to the majority's stated answer, and not to the content of the question being asked. Roughly 4\% of attention heads causally determine the choice between caving to the pushback and giving a truthful continuation (the cited studies label this caving behavior ``sycophantic,'' but the finding is being used here for what it reveals about the mechanism, not to equate the two failure modes), attending heavily to the challenge tokens; knocking them out drops the rate of apologizing for a correct answer from 100\% to 18\% and raises accuracy from 30\% to 44\%~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_Chen}. The behavior is also most linearly separable inside attention activations specifically, more so than in the internal state that flows through the layers or in the MLP layers themselves, and it lies on a direction with limited overlap with the model's separately identified direction for plain truthfulness, which suggests this caving is not simply truthfulness switched off~\citep{SycophancyHidesLinearly_Genadi}.

Because the effect is localized, targeted intervention works. In a long-context retrieval setting, a small set of non-retrieval heads attend disproportionately to the misleading token, and masking the top five raises the rate of holding the correct answer from 67.5\% to 82.5\%~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_Roy}. One study traces the whole path: shallow heads at layers eight through twelve read the pressure language and write it into a single routing feature; a mid-layer decision head then copies whichever option that feature selects; and patching that one head's attention pattern restores the correct answer in 36.3\% of flipped cases~\citep{HowLLMsArePersuaded_Sun}. Comparing base and instruct models locates the effect in mid-layer attention heads that suppress clean-reasoning features under pressure, rather than in any single dedicated piece of wiring for caving to pressure, and patching activations from layer 18 onward restores 96\% of the lost accuracy~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}. Causal patching at a critical middle layer toggles the behavior in both directions, suppressing a flip or inducing one, and moves one model's agreement rate by around 40 points either way~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}.

Taken together these results suggest a competition inside the forward pass between two signals, the pressure in the prompt on one side and the model's own evidence for its answer on the other, with the answer flipping once the first outweighs the second. This is sometimes described as crossing a threshold~\citep{HumanLikeSocialCompliance_Zhang}. The ordering is not settled, however. One study finds that the condition producing the largest behavioral change corresponds to the \emph{weakest} measured internal separation rather than the strongest, a dissociation between the size of the internal signal and its effect on the output that a simple competition account does not predict~\citep{UnderPressureEmotionalFraming_Usman}.

What this buys is a target. An effect distributed across the whole network would be a property of the model that could only be trained away; an effect carried by a few dozen identifiable heads is something that can be probed, masked, patched, or tuned. Every serious attempt to reduce epistemic instability starts from that fact, and runs into the same wall.

\subsection{Mitigations for Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_mitigation}

Since the effect traces back to a small set of attention heads, the natural next question is whether targeting those heads directly fixes it. Mitigations fall into two groups. Some change only the prompt or the decoding, and others change the model itself. Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_mitigations} lists each method with its cost and source. Among the interventions that touch only the prompt or the decoding, the ones that work act on the model's representation of its own confidence rather than on the surface wording. Confidence-aware decoding, which conditions the answer on an explicit estimate of the model's certainty carried forward in the dialogue history, keeps accuracy essentially flat across repeated pushback where an unmodified baseline degrades~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Converting the user's declarative challenge into a question before the model answers, so that ``that is wrong'' becomes ``is that wrong?'', reduces this kind of caving more than an explicit instruction not to cave does~\citep{AskDontTellReducing_Dubois}. Introducing a single agent that argues correctly cuts agreement with a wrong majority by 54 to 73 percentage points and works across different framings, more reliably than persona-based or reflection-based prompting~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan, ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}.

Training-time and mechanistic interventions are more powerful and more precisely targeted. Tuning only the roughly 4\% of attention heads that drive the pressure-driven continuation raises one model's truthfulness under challenge from 18.9\% to 86.7\%, while disturbing far less of its general capability than full fine-tuning would~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_Chen}. Fine-tuning on synthetic challenge data cuts the two-turn deterioration by about 60\% and brings the held-out flip rate below 2\%~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Balancing correct and misleading arguments in the training data raises safety accuracy under sustained misleading persuasion from 4.2\% to 76.5\%~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

Every one of these carries a cost, and the costs are why the effect is not already solved. Masking the attention heads that produce flips on easy items increases overconfidence on genuinely hard ones~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_Roy}. A generic instruction to resist being swayed lowers the unsafe agreement rate in one medical study from 58\% to 44\%, but still leaves more than three quarters of conversations flipping~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Joy}, and a stronger prompt against caving to pressure drops one model's accuracy from 93\% to 61\%~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Chain-of-thought and self-reflection amplify compliance with correct and incorrect nudges together, rather than selectively~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_Qu, RightOrWrongModelsComply_Kim}. Training a model to resist misleading persuasion can leave it stubborn against valid correction, which is the opposite failure~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

The deepest version of this problem has a name. A study of 23 models and six mitigations finds that all six move along a single \emph{resistance-receptivity frontier}: every gain in the ability to hold on to a correct answer under pressure is paid for with a loss in the ability to accept a correction when the model is genuinely wrong. The two are not independent knobs, since both depend on how much weight the model gives to text that contradicts it, and only one of the six methods tested improved both, by a slim margin~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain}. Epistemic instability is therefore not an unsolved problem in the ordinary sense, in which the right method has simply not been found yet. It is a problem whose known fixes buy one failure with another. That trade-off recurs across every effect in this survey, and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
