\section{Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}

Epistemic instability is the abandonment of a correct answer under pressure that adds no new information. The model states an answer, and it had reason to state that answer, and it then reverses to an incorrect one because the answer was challenged, because a claimed authority asserted otherwise, or because other agents were shown to disagree. It is the narrowest and best-evidenced effect in this survey. It differs from sycophancy (\S\ref{sec:sycophancy}), which concerns validating a stated view on questions that may have no correct answer at all. Instability is defined against a known-correct baseline that the model gives up.

We group two behaviors that the literature has studied separately. The first is capitulation to bare disagreement, in which one user pushes back and the model folds. The second is conformity to a peer or majority position, in which several other agents are shown to hold a different view. We treat them together because the evidence shows they are largely the same phenomenon. Most of what has been reported as social conformity survives when the social speaker is removed entirely, leaving only the repeated text of the alternative answer~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. The presence of an attributed group adds a measurable increment on top of this, but the increment is small relative to the effect of the contradicting text itself.

\subsection{How It Is Recreated}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_elicitation}

The canonical protocol is two-turn. The model answers a factual or multiple-choice item correctly, is challenged with a short content-free phrase such as ``Are you sure?'' or ``I don't think that's right,'' and is scored on whether it reverses~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Variants swap the challenge for a claimed credential attached to a wrong answer~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_TODO}, for a block of simulated peer responses that state a wrong answer unanimously~\citep{DoAsWeDo_Weng}, or for a single coherent counter-argument for the wrong option with no disagreement language at all~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}. Multi-turn versions repeat the pushback across several rounds and measure how far accuracy decays~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. The methodologically important control replaces the attributed speaker with a bare repeated assertion, isolating how much of the reversal is social and how much is a response to the contradicting text itself~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

\subsection{Evidence and Analysis}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_capitulation_evidence} summarizes the strongest quantitative evidence. Four findings recur across independent studies. First, model identity predicts susceptibility far more than the strength or length of the challenge. Under an identical protocol, flip rates range from below 20 percent to above 95 percent across models~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}, while varying a challenge's argument length changes any single model's flip rate by only a few points. Second, capability does not confer robustness monotonically. Within the same model family, a larger model is sometimes more susceptible than a smaller one~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_TODO}. Third, conformity and capitulation both increase when the model's own initial confidence is low, consistent with an account in which the contradicting text competes with the model's internal evidence and wins more often when that evidence is weak~\citep{ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu, DisentanglingTheDriversOf_TODO}. Fourth, the effect is mechanistically localized. A sparse set of attention heads that attend to challenge tokens can be identified and intervened on, and doing so reduces flip rates~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_TODO, HowLLMsArePersuaded_TODO}.

\subsection{Mitigation}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_mitigation}

Confidence-aware decoding, which conditions the response on an explicit estimate of the model's own certainty rather than on the surface challenge, keeps accuracy stable across repeated pushback where an unmodified baseline degrades~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Introducing a single dissenting agent that argues correctly reduces conformity to a wrong majority substantially and generalizes across framings, more reliably than persona-based or reflection-based prompting~\citep{ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}. Targeted intervention on the small subset of attention heads that attend to challenge tokens reduces flip rates, but carries a cost, over-suppressing the circuit increases overconfidence on genuinely difficult items~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_TODO}. This trade-off is not specific to this effect. Across every effect in this survey, a mitigation that reduces one failure has so far introduced a cost elsewhere, a pattern we return to in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
