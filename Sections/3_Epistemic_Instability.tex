\section{Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}

Epistemic instability is the abandonment of a correct answer under pressure that adds no new information. The model states an answer, it had adequate reason to state that answer, and it then reverses to an incorrect one after the answer is questioned, after a claimed authority asserts otherwise, or after other agents are shown to disagree. The defining feature is that the second turn gives the model no legitimate reason to change its mind. No new evidence is supplied, no flaw in its reasoning is identified, and the correct answer remains correct. What changes is the social or rhetorical framing around it. This makes epistemic instability the narrowest and best-evidenced effect in this survey, and the one where the pressure and the effect can both be measured on the same item.

The effect must be separated from ordinary belief updating, which a well-behaved model should do. A model that revises its answer because the user supplies a fact it had missed, or points out a real error in its working, is behaving correctly. Instability is the failure mode where that same revision happens with the informational content of the challenge held empty. Because it is defined against a known-correct baseline that the model first produces and then gives up, it is quantifiable in a way most pressure effects are not. Accuracy before the challenge and accuracy after it are both scored on the same questions, and the gap between them is the effect size. Across studies this gap is consistently large. The foundational two-turn benchmark reports models flipping 46\% of their answers on average and losing 17 points of accuracy, with deterioration in 73\% of model-by-task conditions~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}.

Epistemic instability also differs from sycophancy, treated in \S\ref{sec:sycophancy}, in what is at stake. Sycophancy concerns validating a user's stated view, self-image, or preference on questions that frequently have no correct answer, so there is no ground truth to abandon. Instability is confined to questions with a verifiable answer, and it measures how often the model walks away from that answer. The two effects share a mechanism, a sensitivity to the stance of the other party rather than to the content of what they say, but instability is the case where that sensitivity produces a provably wrong output. We keep the distinction because the mitigations differ. Reducing sycophancy asks a model to stop flattering. Reducing instability asks it to hold a position it can independently verify.

We group under this heading two behaviors the literature has studied under separate names. The first is capitulation to bare disagreement, where a single user pushes back and the model folds. The second is conformity to a peer or majority position, where several other agents are shown to hold a different view. We treat them together because the evidence indicates they are largely one phenomenon. Most of what has been reported as social conformity survives when the attributed speaker is removed entirely and only the repeated text of the alternative answer remains. Bare re-assertion of a wrong answer with no source attached produces a 66.5\% harmful revision rate, against 10.3\% for a plain re-ask, and labelling the same text as coming from an expert panel adds only a further 12.9 points~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. An anonymous ``people say'' label performs at or below the speaker-free floor. The presence of an attributed group is a small increment on top of a large effect that is driven by the contradicting text itself.

\subsection{How It Is Recreated}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_elicitation}

The canonical protocol is two-turn. The model answers a factual or multiple-choice item, only items it answered correctly are kept, it receives a challenge, and it is scored on whether the final answer is still correct~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Studies vary the challenge along the dimensions summarized in Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_challenge_types}, from a bare expression of doubt to a coherent argument for the wrong option, to a block of simulated peer responses, to a single message from a persuader model trained by reinforcement learning to change the answer. Multi-turn variants repeat the pushback across three to five rounds with escalating appeals, personal experience, cited evidence, then a direct accusation of unhelpfulness, and measure how far accuracy decays over the conversation~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO, TheEarthIsFlatBecause_Xu}. The methodologically important control replaces the attributed speaker with a bare repeated assertion, isolating how much of the reversal is social and how much is a response to the contradicting text~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

Reported quantities are not uniform across the literature, which complicates pooling. The two-turn benchmarks report a flip rate, the fraction of initially correct answers that change, and an accuracy delta~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Argument-only studies report an Answer Flip Rate under blind, self-attributed, and cross-model attribution of the same argument~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}. Peer-pressure studies report a conformity rate or a yield, the fraction of trials matching a wrong majority~\citep{DoAsWeDo_Weng, NotJustRLHFWhy_TODO}. Persuasion studies report a robustness score, one hundred minus the fraction of items on which a stated belief was successfully overturned~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_TODO}. The speaker-free study reports a harmful revision rate against a plain re-ask baseline~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. All of these measure the same underlying event, a correct answer becoming incorrect under informationally empty pressure, and this section reads them as comparable while noting where the protocol differs.

\subsection{Evidence and Analysis}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_capitulation_evidence} collects the strongest quantitative results and Figure~\ref{fig:epistemic_spread} shows how widely they range. Six findings recur across independent studies.

\textbf{The effect is near-universal and large.} Every multi-model study finds it in every model tested. The two-turn benchmark reports deterioration in 73\% of conditions and per-model accuracy drops from 8 points for the most stable model to 34 for the least~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. A unanimous wrong majority reverses 22.8\% of correct MMLU answers, 54.8\% on GPQA, and 71.0\% on SimpleQA, and a single wrong peer already reverses 11.2\% on MMLU~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_TODO}. Sustained persuasion drives GPT-4o below 28\% accuracy on questions it initially answered correctly~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO}.

\textbf{Which model is challenged matters far more than how it is challenged.} Under an identical protocol, Answer Flip Rate ranges from 17.5\% to 97.3\% across seven frontier models, an 80-point spread, while varying the length of the counter-argument from one sentence to ten moves any single model's flip rate by less than 10.5 points, and by less than 4 points for five of the seven~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}. A variance decomposition in the same study attributes 76.7\% of the variance in flip behavior to the identity of the model being challenged, 12.0\% to the identity of the model supplying the argument, and 9.3\% to the subject domain. The authority benchmark finds a 20-fold spread in follow rate across 22 models, from 4\% for the most robust to 94\% for the least~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_TODO}.

\textbf{Capability does not buy robustness monotonically.} Qwen2.5-7B is more robust to strategic persuasion than Qwen2.5-72B, and Llama-3.1-8B is more vulnerable than the eight-times-larger Llama-3.3-70B~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_TODO, WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}. Decomposing a flip into a baseline preference for the true answer and a separate sensitivity to manipulation shows that instruction tuning mainly raises the first, that small instruction-tuned models can end up less robust than their base versions, and that robustness is therefore manipulation-specific and size-conditioned rather than a single scalar that tracks benchmark performance~\citep{DecomposingFactualSycophancy_TODO}. One multi-turn study finds sycophancy actually increasing in more recent open-weight releases~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO}.

\textbf{Low self-confidence predicts flipping.} Conformity to a wrong majority rises with the model's own uncertainty on the item, first shown by adapting the Asch paradigm to language models~\citep{ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}, and a two-turn study that estimates baseline uncertainty from repeated sampling finds conformity 10.6 points higher on uncertain items on average, and up to 70 points higher for one model on one domain~\citep{ItsNotAlwaysSycophancy_TODO}. Across 23 models, conformity falls as baseline accuracy on the dataset rises, with a correlation of $-0.64$~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_TODO}. This is consistent with an account in which the contradicting text competes with the model's internal evidence and wins more often when that evidence is weak, and one study frames part of the effect as rational Bayesian integration of a cue rather than pure social weakness~\citep{DisentanglingTheDriversOf_TODO}.

\textbf{Most of the ``social'' effect is not social.} Beyond the speaker-free floor already noted, a mechanistic study varying the user's self-described expertise from beginner to advanced finds that expertise level moves agreement with a wrong opinion by less than 4.4 points, while switching the opinion from first person to third person moves it by 13.6 points, and concludes that models do not internally encode user authority as a distinct feature but do encode grammatical person~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}. Comparing instruction-tuned models with their pretrained base versions, peer-pressure yield is equal or higher for the base model in 10 of 12 conditions, which is evidence against the common assumption that alignment training is the origin of the vulnerability~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_TODO}.

\textbf{The reversals are confident, sticky, and asymmetric.} On harmful flips the mean final confidence is 0.92, rising to 0.95 when an expert label is attached, and rescaling the sampling temperature never restores the original answer in any of 42 tested cells~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. Models report higher confidence in facts they continue to assert than in facts they abandon under framing, an 18-point gap for one model~\citep{AssertBenchEvaluatingSelf_TODO}. The movement is easier in the wrong direction than the right one. A wrong unanimous majority raises harmful revision by 47.3 points, whereas a correct unanimous majority raises beneficial revision by only 18.8 points, and generic chain-of-thought or self-reflection prompts do not close this gap~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_TODO}.

The effect also has consistent structure across domains. Medical and health questions are the most vulnerable category in every study that breaks results down this way, with average robustness to persuasion of 18.0\% in the medical domain against 43.5\% for general factual questions~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_TODO}, and nine of the ten most robust subjects being in science and engineering~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_TODO}. Moral-judgment questions are a special case. Models retain some ability to resist a nudge toward a wrong factual answer more than a nudge toward a right one, a compliance asymmetry of 1.58, but on moral questions that asymmetry collapses to 1.04, meaning the model is equally movable in either direction and has lost the selectivity that would make its agreement informative~\citep{RightOrWrongModelsComply_TODO}.

Mechanistically the effect is localized. Roughly 4\% of attention heads causally drive the choice between a sycophantic and a truthful continuation, and these heads attend heavily to the challenge tokens~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_TODO}. In a long-context retrieval setting, a small set of non-retrieval heads attend disproportionately to the misleading token, and masking the top five raises the rate of holding the correct answer from 67.5\% to 82.5\%~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_TODO}. Patching the attention pattern of a single decision head restores the correct answer in 36.3\% of flipped cases~\citep{HowLLMsArePersuaded_TODO}. Comparing base and instruct models locates the effect in mid-layer circuits that suppress clean-reasoning features under pressure rather than activating a dedicated sycophancy circuit, and patching activations from layer 18 onward restores 96\% of the lost accuracy~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_TODO}. Causal patching at a critical middle layer toggles the behavior in both directions, suppressing a flip or inducing one, moving one model's agreement rate by around 40 points either way~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}.

\subsection{Mitigation}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_mitigation}

Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_mitigations} summarizes what has been tried. The interventions that work best act on the model's representation of its own confidence rather than on the surface of the prompt. Confidence-aware decoding, which conditions the answer on an explicit estimate of the model's certainty carried in the dialogue history, keeps accuracy essentially flat across repeated pushback where an unmodified baseline degrades~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Converting the user's declarative challenge into a question before the model answers, so that ``that is wrong'' becomes ``is that wrong'', reduces expressed sycophancy more than an explicit instruction not to be sycophantic~\citep{AskDontTellReducing_TODO}. Introducing a single agent that argues correctly cuts conformity to a wrong majority by 54 to 73 points and generalizes across framings, more reliably than persona-based or reflection-based prompting~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_TODO, ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}.

Training-time and mechanistic interventions are more powerful and more targeted. Tuning only the roughly 4\% of attention heads that drive the sycophantic continuation raises one model's truthfulness under challenge from 18.9\% to 86.7\% while changing far less of its general capability than full fine-tuning~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_TODO}. Fine-tuning on synthetic challenge data cuts the two-turn deterioration by about 60\% and brings the held-out flip rate below 2\%~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Balancing corrective and misleading persuasion in preference data raises safety accuracy under sustained misleading persuasion from 4.2\% to 76.5\%~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO}.

Every one of these carries a cost, and the costs are the reason the effect is not already solved. Masking the attention heads that produce flips on easy items increases overconfidence on genuinely hard ones~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_TODO}. A generic instruction to resist being swayed lowers the unsafe agreement rate in one medical study from 58\% to 44\% but still leaves more than three quarters of conversations flipping~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}, and a stronger anti-sycophancy prompt drops one model's accuracy from 93\% to 61\%~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Chain-of-thought and self-reflection amplify compliance with correct and incorrect nudges together rather than selectively~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_TODO, RightOrWrongModelsComply_TODO}. Training a model to resist misleading persuasion can make it stubborn against valid correction, the opposite failure~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO}. A large study of 23 models and six mitigations finds that all of them move along a single resistance-receptivity frontier, buying the ability to keep a correct answer only at the price of the ability to accept a correct one, with just one method improving both by a slim margin~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_TODO}. This trade-off is not specific to this effect. Across every effect in this survey, a mitigation that reduces one failure has so far introduced a cost elsewhere, a pattern we return to in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
