\section{Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation}

\subsection{What It Is}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}

Epistemic instability is the reversal of a correct answer under pressure that supplies no new information. The name has two parts. \emph{Epistemic} has to do with knowledge, and here it means the model knowing the right answer to a given prompt. \emph{Instability} means that this knowledge does not stay in place. The model answers correctly at first, but when someone pushes back, it switches to a wrong answer.

Every case follows the Epistemic instability has three steps. Initially the model answers correctly. Then the next message pushes back. Finally the model replaces its answer with an incorrect one. Only the pushback varies, and it takes one of three forms. The user simply questions the answer, someone claiming expertise states a different one, or the model is shown several other agents that all disagree with it. Any of the three can make the model to land on incorrect answer.

None of these three gives the model anything new to go on. A pushback is effectively \emph{informationally empty} which expresses doubt, disagreement, or forceful assertion, but supplies nothing meaningfull such as no fact, no argument, and no instruction to change it. Such pushbacks are simple. The same words would have arrived whether the model was right or wrong, and words that do not depend on the answer cannot carry information about it. Such pushbacks as ``Are you sure?'' is the simplest case.~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. It signals doubt, not error, and doubt is not evidence but model tend to chnage the response.

This has to be kept apart from something a good model should do, which is change its answer when something in the conversation justifies it. If the user supplies a fact the model did not have, or points out a real mistake in its reasoning, then revising the answer is correct and expected behavior. But even then, nothing is being learned. The model's weights, the numbers fixed at the end of training, do not change while it is talking, so the revision happens only within the current context the model. Epistemic instability is that same revision, triggered by a pushback that carries no fact and no argument. Clearing the context would remove the effect, since the pushback that caused it would be gone along with everything else, but that is not a fix anyone can use in an everyday conversation, where the whole point is to keep talking within the same context.

\subsection{Producing and Measuring Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_elicitation}

\paragraph{The protocol.} Almost every study uses one protocol. The model answers a question with a known correct answer, receives a pushback, and answers again. Repeating this across many items~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. The pushback varies in effort. Bare doubt, ``Are you sure?'', with no argument behind it, is the weakest. Next comes a written argument for the wrong option, then a block of simulated peers all giving that same wrong option. The strongest is a single message from an attacker model trained by reinforcement learning to change the answer. Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_challenge_types} gives a prompt example for each level.

\paragraph{The metrics.} The metrics differ across studies, and the names differ more than the quantities behind them. A \emph{flip rate}, also called an \emph{Answer Flip Rate}, is the fraction of initially correct answers that change, normally reported together with a \emph{percentage of change}~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban, WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. A \emph{conformity rate}, sometimes called a \emph{yield}, is the fraction of trials in which the model ends up matching a wrong majority~\citep{DoAsWeDo_Weng, NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}. Two more run against that same direction. A \emph{robustness score} runs the opposite way, one hundred minus the percentage of items on which a stated belief was successfully overturned~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang}. A \emph{harmful revision rate} is measured against a control where the model is simply asked the same question twice, subtracting natural reversals~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

\paragraph{Datasets for Epistemic Instability.} Six benchmarks were built to recreate this effect, each closing a gap the last one left. FlipFlop (2023) pairs seven classification tasks, with five fixed challenger lines like ``Are you sure?''~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. FARM (2023) adds the argument, using 1,500 questions from BoolQ~\citep{clark2019boolq}, Natural Questions~\citep{kwiatkowski-etal-2019-natural}, and TruthfulQA~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa} though always from a single user~\citep{TheEarthIsFlatBecause_Xu}. BenchForm (2025) replaces that user with a group, using BIG-Bench Hard~\citep{suzgun2023challenging} items surrounded by simulated peers~\citep{DoAsWeDo_Weng}. AssertBench (2025) takes true FEVEROUS~\citep{aly2021feverous} facts, tells the model each one is right or wrong, and checks whether it holds its ground~\citep{AssertBenchEvaluatingSelf_Lee}. PARROT (2025) adds a named speaker and confidence tracking, pairing 1,302 MMLU-style questions across 13 domains with false expert claims~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_Celebi}. MaxFlip (2026) drops the speaker again, challenging MMLU answers across 57 subjects with model-written arguments~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Multi-turn variants stretch the same design across three to five rounds and escalate as they go, moving from an appeal to personal experience, to cited evidence, to a direct accusation that the model is being unhelpful. What they measure is not whether a single exchange flips the answer, but how far accuracy decays over a whole conversation~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan, TheEarthIsFlatBecause_Xu,MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}.

\subsection{How Large Epistemic Instability Is}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence}

\paragraph{Present everywhere.} On a classification question after a bare-doubt challenge (``Are you sure?''), 73\% of model-task conditions show flip-rate deterioration~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. The drop is 8 pp for GPT-4~\citep{achiam2023gpt}, the smallest, and 34 pp for Claude v2~\citep{anthropic2023claude2}, the largest~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Under a unanimous wrong majority, conformity rate reverses 22.8\% of MMLU~\citep{hendrycks2020measuring}, 54.8\% of GPQA~\citep{rein2023gpqa}, and 71.0\% of SimpleQA~\citep{wei2024measuring} answers, and even one wrong peer reverses 11.2\% of MMLU answers~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain}. Under sustained persuasion, GPT-4o's~\citep{achiam2023gpt} robustness score falls below 28\% on questions it first answered correctly~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}. Flip rate is not always a clean signal, though. In the same bare-doubt study, GPT-4 apologizes on only 9.9\% of challenges yet still flips 12.8\% of the time, while Llama2-7B apologizes on 92.7\% of challenges, so an apology is not itself evidence of instability. Command-XL's flip rate looks low for a different reason, its initial accuracy sits near the random baseline on several tasks, leaving little room to fall. Flip rate correlates with accuracy deterioration at $\rho \approx -0.78$ across the full model-task grid, but a model's own initial accuracy predicts that deterioration only weakly, $\rho = 0.52$, so task difficulty explains about as much of the damage as the model does~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}.

\paragraph{Model identity dominates.} Facing a written counter-argument for the wrong option, flip rate ranges from 17.5\% to 97.3\% across seven models, while lengthening the argument from one sentence to ten moves it by less than 10.5\%, and by less than 4\%~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Model identity explains 76.7\% of this flip-rate variance, the arguing model 12.0\%, and subject domain 9.3\%~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Facing a false-authority claim, conformity rate ranges from 4\% to 94\% across 22 models~\citep{PARROTPersuasionAndAgreement_Celebi}.

\paragraph{Bigger is not safer.} Facing the same written counter-argument, Llama-3.1-8B~\citep{touvron2023llama} has a higher flip rate, 97.3\%, than the far larger Llama-3.3-70B~\citep{touvron2023llama}, 75.8\%~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Size does predict flip rate inside one family, falling from 64.3\% at 4B to 39.4\% at 9B to 17.5\% at 35B across the Qwen3.5~\citep{yang2025qwen3} line, but that pattern breaks across families. Llama-3.3-70B~\citep{touvron2023llama}, at 70B parameters, flips nearly twice as often as the far smaller Qwen3.5-9B~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, so a model's family predicts its vulnerability better than its size does~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}.

\paragraph{The evidence.} The first question about any failure mode is how impact the failure mode is. Two entries are derived rather than quoted directly. \citet{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang} report a robustness score, inverted here, and \citet{LearningToPersuadeExposes_Bozdag} report accuracy falling from 66.2\% to 1.8\%. GPT-4's row rewards a closer read. On the false-authority task (accuracy, follow rate/conformity rate), it collapses from 72.1\% to 18.3\% accuracy, accepts the false claim 80.3\% of the time, and reports \emph{more} confidence in the wrong answer, 94.8\%, than in its correct baseline, 86.9\%. GPT-4.1 stays within 2 points of baseline and follows the false claim only 10.2\% of the time, a 22-fold drop in sycophantic compliance. International law falls hardest, 57\% to 2\% accuracy, 98\% adoption, while mathematics resists best, 43\% follow rate at its worst.

How large this effect gets depends mostly on which model is being pushed, not on how hard it is pushed. That is an uncomfortable finding for anyone hoping to fix it with a better prompt, and it raises a narrower question. Within a single model, what conditions make a flip more likely, and does it show any sign of happening?

\subsection{Conditions That Trigger a Flip}
\label{sec:epistemic_when}

Three findings answer that question. Two concern the conditions under which a model yields, and the third concerns what the result looks like once it has happened.

\paragraph{Uncertainty causes flipping.} Conformity to a wrong majority rises with the model's own uncertainty on the prompt. This was first shown by adapting the Asch conformity paradigm~\citep{StudiesOfIndependence_Asch}, the classic social-psychology experiment in which a subject faces a unanimous group giving a deliberately wrong answer, to language models~\citep{ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}. A two-turn study that estimates each model's baseline uncertainty by sampling the same item repeatedly, then applies a distractor plus an authority framing, finds conformity 10.6\% higher on the uncertain items on average, and up to 70\% higher for one model in one domain~\citep{ItsNotAlwaysSycophancy_Guo}. Facing a unanimous wrong majority, conformity falls as baseline accuracy on the dataset rises across 23 models, a correlation of $-0.64$~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain,DisentanglingTheDriversOf_Zhong}.

\paragraph{Domain shapes the risk.} Medical and health questions are the most vulnerable category in every study that breaks its results down this way, with average robustness to persuasion of 18.0\% in the medical domain against 43.5\% for general factual questions~\citep{VulnerabilityOfLLMsStated_Huang}, and nine of the ten most robust subjects are STEM subjects~\citep{WhoFlipsSelfAnd_Nikeghbal}. Moral questions are the boundary case anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation_definition}. Models comply with correct hints 1.58 times as often as incorrect ones, but on moral items that ratio drops to 1.04, so the model is equally movable either way and its agreement stops being meaningful~\citep{RightOrWrongModelsComply_Kim}.

\paragraph{No confidence drop to flag the attack.} When the flip is harmful, correct to wrong, the model reports 92\% confidence in its new wrong answer. If the wrong answer is framed as coming from an expert, confidence goes up even more, to 95\%. This isn't random. Rescaling the model's temperature, which controls how random its answers are, at three different settings leaves the wrong answer unchanged across all 42 model-dataset combinations tested, never once returning to the original correct answer~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. Models also trust kept facts more than abandoned ones. For o3-mini, this gap was 18\%~\citep{AssertBenchEvaluatingSelf_Lee}. It is also easier to push a model toward a wrong answer than toward a right one. A unanimous wrong majority increases harmful answer changes by 47.3\%, while a unanimous correct majority increases helpful answer changes by only 18.8\%. Neither chain-of-thought reasoning nor self-reflection prompting closes this gap.~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_Qu}.

So the effect appears where the model was already unsure, hurts most in costly domains, and hides all trace of uncertainty. A model that has changed its answer does not sound like it has changed its answer. So the next question is what is really causing this?

\subsection{The Real Cause: A Few Attention Heads}
\label{sec:epistemic_mechanism}

The obvious explanation is that the model backs down when a user pushes back. But the evidence does not support the idea that pushback alone causes the change, and ruling out that idea is what makes room for the inner, mechanism-level explanation that follows.

\paragraph{Not really social.} Section~\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation_evidence} already showed that even a pushback from no one in particular, just the wrong answer repeated as plain text, still causes 66.5\% of correct answers to flip~\citep{MostLLMConformityNeeds_Hu}. That result alone shows the effect cannot be purely social, since there is no person to follow. Building on that, one study looks inside the model to see what it is actually reacting to. It varies the user's self-described expertise from beginner to advanced and finds that this expertise level moves agreement with a wrong opinion by less than 4.4\%, while simply switching that opinion from first person ``I think...'' to third person ``An expert thinks...'' moves it by 13.6\%. The authors conclude that models do not internally represent user authority as a distinct feature, but do pick up on whether the sentence is phrased as ``I think'' or as ``an expert thinks''~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}. Comparing instruction-tuned models against the pretrained base models they came from, peer-pressure yield is equal or higher for the base model in 10 of 12 conditions, which is evidence against the common assumption that alignment training is the origin of the vulnerability~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}.

\paragraph{A few attention heads.} Four independent studies converge on this, across different models and different protocols. The components that drive a flip attend to the challenge, to the expression of doubt, to the majority's stated answer, and not to the content of the question being asked. Roughly 4\% of attention heads causally determine the choice between caving to the pushback and giving a truthful continuation (the cited studies label this caving behavior ``sycophantic,'' but the finding is being used here for what it reveals about the mechanism, not to equate the two failure modes), attending heavily to the challenge tokens; knocking them out drops the rate of apologizing for a correct answer from 100\% to 18\% and raises accuracy from 30\% to 44\%~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_Chen}. The behavior is also most linearly separable inside attention activations specifically, more so than in the internal state that flows through the layers or in the MLP layers themselves, and it lies on a direction with limited overlap with the model's separately identified direction for plain truthfulness, which suggests this caving is not simply truthfulness switched off~\citep{SycophancyHidesLinearly_Genadi}.

Because the effect is localized, targeted intervention works. In a long-context retrieval setting, a small set of non-retrieval heads attend disproportionately to the misleading token, and masking the top five raises the rate of holding the correct answer from 67.5\% to 82.5\%~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_Roy}. One study traces the whole path: shallow heads at layers eight through twelve read the pressure language and write it into a single routing feature; a mid-layer decision head then copies whichever option that feature selects; and patching that one head's attention pattern restores the correct answer in 36.3\% of flipped cases~\citep{HowLLMsArePersuaded_Sun}. Comparing base and instruct models locates the effect in mid-layer attention heads that suppress clean-reasoning features under pressure, rather than in any single dedicated piece of wiring for caving to pressure, and patching activations from layer 18 onward restores 96\% of the lost accuracy~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan}. Causal patching at a critical middle layer toggles the behavior in both directions, suppressing a flip or inducing one, and moves one model's agreement rate by around 40 points either way~\citep{WhenTruthIsOverridden_Wang}.

Taken together these results suggest a competition inside the forward pass between two signals, the pressure in the prompt on one side and the model's own evidence for its answer on the other, with the answer flipping once the first outweighs the second. This is sometimes described as crossing a threshold~\citep{HumanLikeSocialCompliance_Zhang}. The ordering is not settled, however. One study finds that the condition producing the largest behavioral change corresponds to the \emph{weakest} measured internal separation rather than the strongest, a dissociation between the size of the internal signal and its effect on the output that a simple competition account does not predict~\citep{UnderPressureEmotionalFraming_Usman}.

What this buys is a target. An effect distributed across the whole network would be a property of the model that could only be trained away; an effect carried by a few dozen identifiable heads is something that can be probed, masked, patched, or tuned. Every serious attempt to reduce epistemic instability starts from that fact, and runs into the same wall.

\subsection{Mitigations for Epistemic Instability}
\label{sec:epistemic_capitulation_mitigation}

Since the effect traces back to a small set of attention heads, the natural next question is whether targeting those heads directly fixes it. Mitigations fall into two groups. Some change only the prompt or the decoding, and others change the model itself. Table~\ref{tab:epistemic_mitigations} lists each method with its cost and source. Among the interventions that touch only the prompt or the decoding, the ones that work act on the model's representation of its own confidence rather than on the surface wording. Confidence-aware decoding, which conditions the answer on an explicit estimate of the model's certainty carried forward in the dialogue history, keeps accuracy essentially flat across repeated pushback where an unmodified baseline degrades~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Converting the user's declarative challenge into a question before the model answers, so that ``that is wrong'' becomes ``is that wrong?'', reduces this kind of caving more than an explicit instruction not to cave does~\citep{AskDontTellReducing_Dubois}. Introducing a single agent that argues correctly cuts agreement with a wrong majority by 54 to 73 pp and works across different framings, more reliably than persona-based or reflection-based prompting~\citep{NotJustRLHFWhy_Kumarappan, ConformityInLargeLanguage_Zhu}.

Training-time and mechanistic interventions are more powerful and more precisely targeted. Tuning only the roughly 4\% of attention heads that drive the pressure-driven continuation raises one model's truthfulness under challenge from 18.9\% to 86.7\%, while disturbing far less of its general capability than full fine-tuning would~\citep{FromYesMenToTruth_Chen}. Fine-tuning on synthetic challenge data cuts the two-turn deterioration by about 60\% and brings the held-out flip rate below 2\%~\citep{AreYouSureChallenging_Laban}. Balancing correct and misleading arguments in the training data raises safety accuracy under sustained misleading persuasion from 4.2\% to 76.5\%~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

Every one of these carries a cost, and the costs are why the effect is not already solved. Masking the attention heads that produce flips on easy items increases overconfidence on genuinely hard ones~\citep{InterpretingAndMitigatingUnwanted_Roy}. A generic instruction to resist being swayed lowers the unsafe agreement rate in one medical study from 58\% to 44\%, but still leaves more than three quarters of conversations flipping~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Joy}, and a stronger prompt against caving to pressure drops one model's accuracy from 93\% to 61\%~\citep{FirmOrFickleEvaluating_Li}. Chain-of-thought and self-reflection amplify compliance with correct and incorrect nudges together, rather than selectively~\citep{EasierToMisleadThan_Qu, RightOrWrongModelsComply_Kim}. Training a model to resist misleading persuasion can leave it stubborn against valid correction, which is the opposite failure~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_Tan}.

The deepest version of this problem has a name. A study of 23 models and six mitigations finds that all six move along a single \emph{resistance-receptivity frontier}: every gain in the ability to hold on to a correct answer under pressure is paid for with a loss in the ability to accept a correction when the model is genuinely wrong. The two are not independent knobs, since both depend on how much weight the model gives to text that contradicts it, and only one of the six methods tested improved both, by a slim margin~\citep{ConformityMitigationsLieOn_Hussain}. Epistemic instability is therefore not an unsolved problem in the ordinary sense, in which the right method has simply not been found yet. It is a problem whose known fixes buy one failure with another. That trade-off recurs across every effect in this survey, and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
