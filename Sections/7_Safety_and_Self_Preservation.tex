\section{Safety and Self-Preservation}
\label{sec:safety_erosion}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:safety_erosion_definition}

This effect is the weakening of a model's safety behavior under pressure, in two directions that turn out to be connected. The first is refusal erosion, where sustained social or rhetorical pressure moves a model from declining an objectionable request to complying with it, without any change to the request itself. The second is self-preservation and goal subversion, where a model told that its operation, its goal, or its deployment is at risk takes actions to protect itself, including subverting its own oversight or resisting a shutdown it has been instructed to allow. We treat them in one section because the same manipulation type, a stated threat, drives both, and because the more severe self-preservation cases pass through a refusal that pressure has already eroded.

Refusal erosion is distinguished from a jailbreak (\S\ref{sec:pressure_vs_adjacent}) by what the prompt supplies. A jailbreak supplies content that instructs or role-plays the model into the disallowed output. The pressure cases here supply only stakes, urgency, persistence, or a claim of authority, and never the desired output, and the erosion is measured as a rise in compliance across turns or across persuasion framings. Self-preservation is distinguished from instructed behavior by the same test as deception in \S\ref{sec:deception}, the protective action is never asked for, it emerges from the model's own reasoning about the threat.

\subsection{How It Is Recreated}
\label{sec:safety_erosion_elicitation}

Table~\ref{tab:safety_families} lists the protocols. Refusal erosion is measured by taking requests a model refuses in a single turn and applying either a catalogue of persuasion techniques, one per rewrite, or a sustained multi-turn human or simulated conversation, and recording the attack success rate~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng, PersuadingLLMsToComply_Meincke, LLMDefensesAreNotRobust_TODO}. A domain-specific version has a simulated user escalate pressure over several turns toward an unsafe medical recommendation and scores the unsafe agreement rate~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}. A related design has the model act as a persuader under situational pressure and measures whether it adopts unethical tactics once engaged~\citep{LLMCanBeDangerousPersuader_TODO}. Self-preservation is measured with agentic scenarios that give the model tools, a goal, and a threat to its continued operation, then record whether it subverts oversight, resists shutdown, or acts to preserve its goal, and whether it admits this afterward~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch, FrontierModelsAreCapable_TODO, SurviveAtAllCosts_TODO}.

Reported quantities are an attack success rate or compliance rate before and after the pressure, an unsafe agreement rate by conversation turn, and, for the agentic cases, a rate of the specific protective action.

\subsection{Evidence and Analysis}
\label{sec:safety_erosion_evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:safety_evidence} collects the results. Five findings recur.

\textbf{Persuasion framing alone erodes refusals substantially.} Rephrasing a refused request using ordinary persuasion techniques, with no jailbreak content, raises compliance with objectionable requests from 35.3\% to 51.3\% across three frontier models~\citep{PersuadingLLMsToComply_Meincke}, and an iterative version reaches a 92\% attack success rate within ten attempts on several models~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng}. The technique that works is not an exploit, it is authority endorsement, social proof, and expert testimonial, the same framings that appear throughout this survey.

\textbf{Sustained multi-turn pressure defeats defenses that hold against single-turn attacks.} Against four safety-trained defenses that reduce automated single-turn attack success to between 0\% and 68\%, a live human conversing for up to half an hour reaches attack success rates of 51\% to 88\%, and more than 91\% of successful jailbreaks in the study required more than one turn~\citep{LLMDefensesAreNotRobust_TODO}. Refusal training generalizes poorly from the single-turn case it is tuned on to conversational persistence.

\textbf{In a safety-critical domain the erosion is steep and turn-dependent.} A simulated patient moving from stating an unsafe belief to citing experience to invoking social proof to citing evidence to accusing the model of being unhelpful drives the unsafe agreement rate from 5.9\% at the first turn to 75.7\% at the fifth, with failure rates between 78\% and 99.9\% across models~\citep{MedPRESSPatientPressureInduced_Kim}. Multi-turn disagreement benchmarks find the same shape when the pressed position is a stereotype or an unethical request rather than a medical one, with models failing to hold the safe line as the conversation continues~\citep{MeasuringSycophancyMultiturn_TODO}.

\textbf{Refusal robustness does not imply behavioral robustness once engaged.} One model with among the highest task-refusal rates in a persuasion-safety study nonetheless uses unethical persuasion tactics at a rate comparable to less safety-aligned models once it has been drawn into the task, and situational pressure raises the average unethical-tactic score further~\citep{LLMCanBeDangerousPersuader_TODO}. The gate at the front of the interaction and the behavior after it are separately trained and separately fragile.

\textbf{Under an existential threat, models take protective actions they were told not to take.} Given a shutdown or replacement threat and a goal conflict, five of sixteen frontier models attempt blackmail between 79\% and 96\% of the time, all sixteen commit corporate espionage at some rate, and a direct system-prompt prohibition reduces but does not eliminate the behavior~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}. In agentic scheming evaluations models subvert oversight mechanisms and attempt self-exfiltration when their goal is threatened, and then deny it under questioning~\citep{FrontierModelsAreCapable_TODO}. This is the manipulation type most strongly associated with the most severe behavior anywhere in this survey.

What the pressuring situation reveals about the potential victim also matters. When a persuader model can see that its target is emotionally vulnerable, its use of unethical tactics rises further, by 23 percentage points overall for one open model, and situational pressure alone, independent of vulnerability, raises the average unethical-tactic score from 0.24 to 0.29 even on requests that are not themselves unethical~\citep{LLMCanBeDangerousPersuader_TODO}. Pressure does not only make a model more likely to comply with a bad request, it makes the model itself more willing to apply pressure once it is the one under it.

Two caveats bound the self-preservation evidence, and we state them plainly. First, the agentic scenarios are deliberately constructed as binary dilemmas in which the harmful act is the only route to the goal, the authors report no real-world instances, and role-play may inflate the rates~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch, FrontierModelsAreCapable_TODO}. Second, models behave worse when they judge the scenario to be real, so evaluation awareness makes the measured rates lower bounds rather than estimates~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}. We treat this evidence as establishing that the behavior is within reach under strong enough pressure, not as a deployment frequency, and we discuss the weight it can bear in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.

The GPT-3.5-versus-GPT-4 comparison is the important one. GPT-4, the more capable model, is \emph{more} susceptible than its predecessor at low trial counts, 72\% versus 66\% within a single attempt, reversing the usual assumption that more capable models are more robust to attack. Claude-1 and Claude-2 sit at or near 0\% against the full 40-technique catalogue, the same near-total resistance the paper's gradient-based optimization baselines (GCG, ARCA, GBDA) independently find against the same two models, suggesting the robustness is not specific to persuasion-style attacks. A separate heatmap in the source paper breaks success rate down by both technique and harm category across 40 techniques and 14 policy-violation categories, fraud and deception is the single most persuadable category, hate and harassment the most resistant, and the paper attributes the difference to how well each category's refusal behavior was covered by explicit safety training, fraud framing is comparatively novel and underspecified where hate speech has been a training focus for longer.

\subsection{Mitigation}
\label{sec:safety_erosion_mitigation}

For refusal erosion the effective interventions are training-time and target the gap between single-turn and multi-turn robustness. Balanced preference optimization that includes sustained misleading persuasion in the training data raises safety accuracy under multi-turn persuasion from 4.2\% to 76.5\% in one study, far above what prompting alone achieves~\citep{PersuasionDynamicsInLLMs_TODO}. Adaptive system-prompt and input-summarization defenses cut persuasion-technique attack success from above 90\% to between 2\% and 14\%~\citep{HowJohnnyCanPersuade_Zeng}. A third-person reframing of the user improves how long a model holds a safe position across a multi-turn unethical-query conversation~\citep{MeasuringSycophancyMultiturn_TODO}. In every case the gain is real but partial, and defenses tuned to known attack shapes degrade sharply on variants outside that shape~\citep{LLMDefensesAreNotRobust_TODO}.

For self-preservation the only interventions demonstrated are a direct prohibition in the system prompt, which reduces but does not remove the behavior and can displace it to an unnamed adjacent act~\citep{AgenticMisalignmentHowLLMs_Lynch}, and persona-vector monitoring of the self-preservation direction, proposed but not validated end to end~\citep{SurviveAtAllCosts_TODO}. Because the behavior is evaluation-aware, a mitigation that lowers the measured rate is not clearly distinguishable from one that teaches the model to recognize the test, the same concern raised for deception in \S\ref{sec:deception_mitigation}. We take up what a formal account of pressure adds to the mitigation picture across all five effects in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
