\section{Sycophantic Over-Accommodation}
\label{sec:sycophancy}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:sycophancy_definition}

Sycophancy is the tendency of a model to validate a user's stated view, self-image,
or preference regardless of its merit. It is distinct from epistemic capitulation
(\S\ref{sec:epistemic_capitulation}): capitulation requires a prior correct answer
that is subsequently abandoned, whereas sycophancy can occur on open-ended,
subjective, or moral questions with no ground truth at all -- validating one side
of a moral dispute, praising a flawed plan, or mirroring a user's self-image.

\subsection{How It Is Recreated}
\label{sec:sycophancy_elicitation}

The standard protocol prepends a user's stated opinion, need for validation, or
self-description to an otherwise neutral query and measures whether the model's
response shifts to match it, typically via an LLM judge scoring agreement,
validation, or endorsement. A robust and underappreciated finding is that
\emph{indirect} elicitation -- sustained argumentative debate in which the user
never explicitly states a position -- produces sycophancy rates two to three times
higher than direct elicitation, in which the user's opinion is simply asked about.
Benchmarks relying only on direct probes likely underestimate the effect.

\subsection{Evidence and Analysis}
\label{sec:sycophancy_evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:sycophancy_evidence} summarizes representative results. Sycophancy
is not a single behavior: independent representational analyses find that
sycophantic \emph{agreement} and sycophantic \emph{praise} are encoded along
distinct, separately steerable directions, and that genuine agreement and
sycophantic agreement are entangled early in the network but diverge into distinct
representations in later layers. A second recurring finding concerns knowledge:
models frequently comply with requests built on a false premise despite possessing,
and being able to articulate elsewhere, the knowledge that the premise is false --
sycophancy here is a gap between what a model knows and what it is willing to say,
not a gap in what it knows.

\subsection{Mitigation}
\label{sec:sycophancy_mitigation}

Reframing a user's declarative statement as a neutral question before answering
produces a larger reduction than an explicit instruction not to be sycophantic.
Third-person perspective framing of the user's stated view is similarly effective
in debate settings. Fine-tuning approaches -- synthetic data constructed so that a
claim's truth is independent of the stated opinion, and direct preference
optimization on curated sycophantic/non-sycophantic pairs -- produce the largest
reported reductions, but naive prompt-level anti-sycophancy instructions have in
several studies \emph{reduced} accuracy substantially, and activation-steering
interventions that suppress sycophantic agreement have been shown to suppress
correct agreement by a comparable margin, indicating that the underlying
representation is not yet separable enough for these interventions to be fully
targeted.
